---
layout: post
title: Pursue "Freedom From" Over "Freedom To"
date: 2026-03-11
---

Most people, when they think about freedom, think about what they want to be able to do. A higher salary so they can afford more. A promotion so they can make bigger decisions. A new skill so they can access new opportunities. This is "freedom to" - the accumulation of capabilities and options.

The problem with "freedom to" is that it almost always comes packaged with new constraints. A promotion gives you influence but adds meetings and politics. A bigger apartment gives you space but locks in higher rent and maintenance expenses. You're trading one set of limitations for another. Sometimes it's a good trade. Often, it's a wash - you just can't see it because you're focused on the new capability and blind to the new dependency.

There's also freedom from. Freedom from driving a good car. Freedom from having a high social status. This is harder to see because it's the absence of something. Your reward system is tuned for acquisition, so subtraction never feels like progress - even when it's the higher-leverage move.

But "freedom from" works differently. Remove one constraint and everything downstream benefits. Freedom from driving a good car reduces your financial anxiety. Freedom from having a high social status reduces your "obligation" to chase. One subtraction, multiple areas of your life get lighter.

You also adapt to new capabilities fast. The new salary feels normal within months. The new access becomes baseline. You need the next thing. But freedom from a chronic stressor doesn't normalize the same way. Six months after dropping something that weighed on you, you still notice the absence. "Freedom to" requires constant replenishment; "freedom from" shifts your baseline.

The default is always accumulation. Everything around you pushes you toward adding: more credentials, more income, more connections, more commitments. Subtraction requires active effort against that current. Saying no to an opportunity that looks good on paper. Dropping a commitment others expect you to keep. Every subtraction feels like you're leaving value on the table - because you are, in one narrow dimension. The question is whether what you gain across all other dimensions is worth more.

Usually it is. You just can't see it at the moment of decision.
