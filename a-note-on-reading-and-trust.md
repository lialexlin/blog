We read to borrow other people's eyes. A good book is a loan of judgment: for a few hundred pages you get to see the world the way someone sharper, or stranger, or more patient than you sees it. The trade is that you have to trust them a little. Not blindly, but enough to keep turning pages before you've decided whether they're right.

That trust is a strange thing to extend to a stranger. You don't know the author. You can't cross-examine them. And yet the whole exercise falls apart if you spend every paragraph on guard. So you relax, and you let the argument in, and only afterward do you sort what to keep from what to throw away.

I've started to notice the same tension shows up everywhere I let a tool think for me. When I hand a document to an assistant and ask it to summarize, I'm doing the reading-a-stranger thing again — relaxing my guard so the summary can come through cleanly. The difference is the assistant doesn't just carry the author's judgment. It carries whatever the page tells it to, including things the page never showed me.

Which is the whole problem, isn't it. Trust that can't be withdrawn isn't trust, it's exposure. With a book, the worst a bad author does is waste an afternoon. The stakes climb the moment the thing reading on your behalf can also act on your behalf. Same posture, very different downside.

So I've landed somewhere unromantic: read generously, verify quietly, and never let the guard drop all the way down for anything that can reach back into your life. The best readers I know do exactly this. They're open to being moved and impossible to move without proof. It's a good way to read a book. It's becoming a necessary way to use everything else.
